1. Feature Extraction from MEMS Vibration Data for Machine Learning
Time-domain features. The standard statistical set—RMS, kurtosis, crest factor, peak-to-peak, skewness, shape factor and standard deviation—is the classic entry point. Sharma et al. built an ADXL1002 + Nucleo-F401RE MEMS system on a gearbox and extracted RMS, kurtosis, crest factor, skewness, shape factor and standard deviation, showing that faulty bearings produce "sudden spikes" in these parameters versus healthy baselines [1]. Notably, in their data the RMS rose from ~1.15 to ~1.99, kurtosis from ~2.2 to ~8.6, and crest factor from ~4.2 to ~12.6 when an inner-raceway fault was introduced—demonstrating that crest factor and kurtosis are especially sensitive to impulsive bearing defects, while RMS tracks overall energy/severity [1]. Pedotti et al. likewise emphasize that RMS, peak, peak-to-peak, crest factor and kurtosis are the standard time-domain diagnostic options, with RMS rising as an overall "noise/energy" indicator of degradation [2].

Frequency-domain features. For localizing faults, FFT-based spectral energy and characteristic defect frequencies (BPFO, BPFI, BSF, FTF) are the key features. Sharma et al. computed theoretical and measured BPFI at ~238.9 Hz (30 Hz shaft) and ~398.1 Hz (50 Hz shaft) and identified harmonics and sidebands around these frequencies as unambiguous inner-raceway indicators [1]. The structure of a bearing fault spectrum is captured by the modulation relationship f = m·fᵢ + n·fᵣ (fault characteristic frequency fᵢ modulated by shaft rotation fᵣ), and the presence of sidebands indicates severity [1]. Pagar et al. give the closed-form BPFO/BPFI/BSF expressions and stress that a defective bearing rotating at constant RPM generates a specific vibration frequency, so peak amplitudes coinciding with the defect frequencies (plus harmonics and sidebands) confirm the fault type and location [14]. Kumari et al. extracted twelve FFT-derived features (mean, standard deviation, maximum and energy for each of the X/Y/Z axes) and fed them to an ANN, achieving 97.21% speed-classification accuracy on a low-cost ESP32 + ADXL335 node [13].

ISO 10816 / ISO 20816 velocity severity. Pedotti et al. explicitly cite ISO 10816-1 as the reference for permissible vibration levels to assist fault diagnosis, using broadband RMS velocity as the severity metric [2]. Pagar et al. use ISO 10816 alarm thresholds and ISO 13373 sensor-placement rules (perpendicular to the shaft, on a clean, grease-free surface close to the bearing) as the framework for their edge alarm system [14]. Di Maggio et al. adopted the ISO 20816-1/-3 benchmark of 4.5 mm/s RMS velocity as a condition-monitoring alert threshold [5]. A critical architectural caveat from Pagar et al. and Di Maggio et al. is that ISO severity charts were not designed for early bearing-degradation detection—bearing defects usually appear at much higher frequencies and amplitudes than imbalance/looseness, so ISO velocity thresholds alone are insufficient and must be complemented by FFT defect-frequency analysis or ML novelty detection [5,14].

Takeaway for your node: compute a compact feature vector combining (a) time-domain statistics (RMS, kurtosis, crest factor, peak-to-peak, skewness), (b) FFT spectral energy in selected bands and the amplitudes at BPFO/BPFI/BSF harmonics, and (c) an ISO 10816/20816 broadband RMS velocity band for an interpretable severity flag. This matches the feature sets used in [1], [2], [5], [13], [14].

2. Edge-Computing Limitations: Is ADXL345 Bandwidth (≤3.2 kHz ODR) Sufficient for Early Bearing Fault Detection?
The fundamental numbers. The ADXL345 is a capacitive MEMS accelerometer with a bandwidth of 0.05–1600 Hz and a maximum output data rate of 3200 Hz [7]. This means that even at the maximum ODR, valid spectral content is limited to roughly 1600 Hz (Nyquist), and—crucially—the practical data rate is further constrained by the bus: over I²C (400 kHz max), the achievable ODR is capped at about 800 Hz [7]. So a realistic ADXL345 streaming implementation sees only the low end of the vibration spectrum.

Why this matters for BPFI/BPFO/BSF. Bearing fault impulses are broadband and excite structural resonances "at a much higher frequency" than the vibrations of other components; the envelope/demodulation approach specifically targets a band around the bearing resonance (typically thousands of Hz) because the low-magnitude, broad-spectrum impulse energy is buried in noise in the raw spectrum [1]. Wang et al. state plainly that the resonance frequency "can be thousands of Hertz" and that the sampling frequency must be several times the resonance frequency per Nyquist, which is why their IIoT node used a MEMS accelerometer (ADXL1001: 0–11 kHz bandwidth, ±100 g, 20 mV/g) sampled at 10 kHz [10]. Sharma et al. quantified this directly: their ADXL335 (±3 g, 550 Hz bandwidth) was unsuitable for high-frequency analysis and only matched a reference piezoelectric system when replaced by an ADXL1002 (±50 g, 21 kHz bandwidth), with the ADC/MCU needing 12-bit resolution and 84 MHz clock to sustain a 5 kHz sampling rate [1]. Sharma et al. also note low-cost MEMS historically had limits up to ~2.5 kHz and ±2 g, requiring higher-bandwidth devices for parity with piezo systems [1].

What the ADXL345 can and cannot do.

Can do: capture shaft-rate phenomena (imbalance, misalignment, looseness) and low-order BPFO/BPFI/BSF components when the machine runs at low-to-moderate speed. Pedotti et al. demonstrated successful unbalance diagnosis (fundamental-frequency amplitude proportional to added mass) using a low-cost MEMS device in the 60–270 rpm range [2]. For a 3000 rpm (50 Hz) shaft, typical BPFI ≈ 5–8×, BPFO ≈ 3–5×, BSF ≈ 2× shaft speed, i.e., roughly 100–400 Hz [1,14]—these fundamental defect frequencies fall within the ADXL345's 1.6 kHz band.
Cannot do reliably: capture the high-frequency resonance carrier (multi-kHz) needed for classical high-frequency envelope/demodulation, nor the higher harmonics that give early-stage sensitivity and noise immunity [1,10]. Consequently, the ADXL345 is marginal for early spall detection (small, incipient BPFI/BPFO/BSF), which is precisely the regime where predictive maintenance adds value.
Practical validation of the ceiling. In the ESP32-based wireless prototype of Kumari et al., the practical, stable sampling rate was only ~1000 Hz; attempts above ~3000 Hz caused packet loss and degraded interrupt responsiveness [13]. The edge-cloud benchmark of Verma et al. used an ADXL345 sampled at 1600 Hz for a Raspberry Pi node, generating ~350 KB per 10-second window [9]. These independent studies confirm that low-cost MEMS + MCU + consumer bus bandwidth caps real-world sampling on the order of 1–3 kHz, matching the ADXL345 datasheet limit [7,9,13].

Architectural conclusion. If your target is early bearing-fault detection (incipient BPFO/BPFI/BSF), the ADXL345 at ≤3.2 kHz ODR is a limiting component. Three options emerge from the literature: (i) migrate to a higher-bandwidth MEMS accelerometer (e.g., ADXL1001/ADXL1002 class, ~11–21 kHz) as demonstrated in [1] and [10], which directly recovered envelope sidebands for inner-race faults; (ii) restrict the ADXL345 to low-speed machinery and to shaft-related faults plus gross severity trending via ISO velocity [2,14]; or (iii) apply envelope/demodulation on the node to shift the fault features into the retained low-frequency band before downsampling—the approach used by Wang et al. to achieve ~95% data reduction while still diagnosing outer- and inner-race faults under variable speed [10].

3. Supervised (XGBoost / Random Forest) vs. Unsupervised (Autoencoder / Isolation Forest) When No Run-to-Failure Labels Exist
The core problem—missing labels. Di Maggio et al. state it directly: "real-world industrial settings often lack structured fault-labeled datasets, making supervised classification approaches impractical," and unsupervised novelty detection is "the most viable alternative" because it trains on normal data only [5]. They further note that fault-diagnosis algorithms typically require substantial labeled data covering many damage and operating states, which is rarely available [5]. Pagar et al. similarly observe that a classifier trained on one dataset/bearing may not generalize to bearings on other equipment with different operating conditions, making universal supervised training impractical [14].

Performance evidence—unsupervised. Di Maggio et al. benchmarked Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM, Local Outlier Factor (LOF) and Elliptic Envelope against a fixed ISO 20816 threshold on medium-sized spherical bearings under variable speed/load, training only on normal data and testing with 10% anomalies [5]. Key results: the fixed ISO threshold generated excessive false positives (poor precision), making it unsuitable for automated monitoring; Isolation Forest achieved the highest recall (detected the most true anomalies) but at the cost of many false positives, while LOF achieved the highest precision, accuracy, F1-score and PR-AUC—the best precision/recall balance and the most conservative (fewest false alarms) model [5]. Zair et al. show that an autoencoder (trained unsupervised on segmented raw time-domain data) combined with t-SNE and a multi-kernel CNN produced a robust health indicator reaching 99.46% test accuracy on CWRU multi-fault data, clearly separating 13 fault states that overlapped when using manual RMS/crest-factor features [8]. This demonstrates the autoencoder's value for learning discriminative features without labels [8].

Performance evidence—supervised. Roberts & Ahmed deployed compact XGBoost and RF ensembles at the edge with model compression and online incremental learning; XGBoost consistently outperformed RF, reaching 98.5% accuracy / F1 ≈ 0.75 on AI4I and 96.5% / F1 ≈ 0.86 on NASA C-MAPSS, with sub-millisecond inference (0.17 ms vs ~100 ms cloud), false-alarm rates <3%, and drift recovery via on-device adaptation [6]. Kumari et al. compared logistic regression, decision tree, RF, KNN, SVM, XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM and an ANN on FFT features from a low-cost ESP32/ADXL335 node; all tree ensembles clustered near 97%, with the ANN highest at 97.21% [13]. Both supervised studies, however, relied on labeled fault datasets [6,13]—the exact condition that real plants often lack [5].

Architectural conclusion for a label-scarce deployment. The literature supports a two-stage / hybrid architecture:

Cold-start (no fault labels): train an autoencoder on healthy baseline data for reconstruction-error anomaly scoring [8], and/or use Isolation Forest for high-recall novelty capture with LOF as a high-precision confirmation model to minimize false alarms [5]. Avoid relying on static ISO thresholds alone—they over-trigger [5].
Warm phase (labels accumulate from confirmed events): migrate to supervised XGBoost/RF for classification and RUL, exploiting their high accuracy and edge efficiency; XGBoost is the stronger single model, and both support on-device incremental learning to track sensor/concept drift without cloud retraining [6,13].
This staged design mirrors the evidence: unsupervised novelty detection is the practical fallback when only normal data exist [5,8], while supervised ensembles deliver the best accuracy and latency once labels are available [6,13].

4. LoRaWAN Payload Optimization for Vibration Telemetry: Feature Vectors vs. Raw Waveform
Why payload size dominates. In wireless sensor nodes, data communication consumes the largest share of energy, and LoRa—despite being low-power and long-range—has notably long transmission timeframes (the LoPy/LoRa node drew 154 mA and needed ~1190 ms to send 14 bytes) [11]. Consequently, reducing transmitted bytes has an outsized effect on node lifetime: applying compression (modified static Huffman + extrapolation prediction + bit aggregation) yielded ~31% energy improvement for LoRa versus ~20% for ZigBee and only ~7% for Enhanced ShockBurst—because the gain scales with transmission timeframe, and LoRa's is the longest [11].

Raw-waveform transmission is impractical; feature/summary telemetry is standard. The LoRaWAN vibration sensor of Katsoulis et al. transmits only a compact structured payload (altitude, gas, humidity, pressure, state-of-charge, a vibration-detected flag, temperature) and uses the accelerometer mainly as a wake-up/interrupt trigger rather than streaming raw data; deep-sleep between events drops consumption from 100–120 mA to ~1–2 µA [3]. This is the archetypal LoRaWAN architecture: transmit derived summaries and event flags, not raw waveforms [3].

If waveform transmission is unavoidable, compress on the node. Wang et al. implemented on-node amplitude demodulation, downsampling and signal mixing (vibration + rotation-trigger multiplexed into one stream) that reduced transmitted data by ~95% while preserving outer- and inner-race diagnosis under variable speed, and the algorithm ran on a low-cost 64 MHz MCU [10]. Alternatively, if reconstruction of the waveform is needed, Tang et al.'s speech-codec-based scheme achieved compression ratios of 4.0–24.6 (AMR-VDC up to 24.6), MSE < 0.008, per-sample compression time ≤0.0194 ms, and preserved tool-wear and bearing-fault diagnostic accuracy (>99% on CWRU after decompression)—i.e., lossy compression that does not destroy fault features [4]. Bengherbia et al. used MFCC preprocessing to convert 1D vibration into a compact 2D feature matrix on an FPGA, enabling both dimensionality reduction "for transmission and storage" and <40 ms on-device inference [12].

Feature-vector sizing vs. LoRaWAN budget. Feature-vector payloads fit comfortably in LoRaWAN's per-message limits. A representative vector—say 6 time-domain statistics + 4 spectral-band energies + BPFO/BPFI/BSF amplitudes × 2 bytes each—occupies roughly 20–60 bytes, well within a single LoRaWAN uplink frame, whereas even a one-second raw waveform at 1–3 kHz is several KB and would require many frames [9,10,13]. Tang et al. formally frame the compression problem as a multi-objective optimization over compression ratio (≥4 required), reconstruction quality (diagnosis-accuracy loss ≤5%), and compression efficiency (<0.5 s per 10,000 samples), which is a useful specification template for your on-node compressor [4].

Where to compute. Verma et al. showed that the optimal edge-vs-cloud split depends on algorithm cost: time-domain features are cheaper to compute at the edge (lower end-to-end latency), whereas FFT was faster in the cloud in their benchmark because edge compute overhead (interrupts, scheduling) dominated—so offloading decision depends on the total edge compute time versus cloud transmission+retrieval overheads [9]. Practically, this argues for extracting a rich feature vector at the edge (cheap) and transmitting only summaries, while keeping heavier spectral/model operations in the backend—consistent with the edge-to-cloud split in your architecture [3,6,9,10].

Consolidated Architectural Recommendations for Your Node
Feature set: transmit a compact vector of time-domain statistics (RMS, kurtosis, crest factor, peak-to-peak, skewness), FFT band energies, BPFO/BPFI/BSF harmonic amplitudes, and the ISO 10816/20816 RMS velocity severity value—rather than raw waveforms [1,2,5,13,14].
Sensor sufficiency: the ADXL345 (≤3.2 kHz ODR; ~800 Hz practical over I²C; ~1–1.6 kHz in real MCU/Wi-Fi prototypes) is adequate for shaft-related faults and gross severity trending, but is marginal for early BPFI/BPFO/BSF detection because it cannot capture the multi-kHz resonance used for envelope analysis; consider an ~11–21 kHz-class MEMS accelerometer or on-node demodulation/downsampling if early fault sensitivity is required [1,7,9,10,13].
Modeling pipeline: begin with unsupervised novelty detection (autoencoder reconstruction error + Isolation Forest for recall, LOF for precision) since run-to-failure labels are typically absent; migrate to XGBoost/RF supervised classification once labels accrue, using on-device incremental learning for drift [5,6,8].
LoRaWAN payload: send feature/summary telemetry and event flags; if raw data must move, compress on the node (demodulate + downsample, or codec-based lossy compression), which achieved ~95% data reduction and ~31% LoRa energy savings respectively, without degrading diagnosis [3,4,10,11].
Edge/cloud split: compute cheap time-domain features on the ESP32-S3 and push heavier spectral/ML workloads to the backend, deciding offload per algorithm cost and latency budget [3,6,9,10,12].
